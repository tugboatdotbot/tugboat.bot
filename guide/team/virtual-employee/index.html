What is it like to add an AI colleague to a small team?

For: the owner of a small business, and the manager or IT person deciding whether to try one. Last checked: November 11, 2026.

The short version

Section titled “The short version”
  • A virtual employee is a new member of staff that is an AI. It has a name, its own chat account and one job. In everyday business use the phrase often means a remote human assistant; on this site it always means an AI.
  • Colleagues message it and hand it work. It keeps the team’s promises from slipping, posts the morning list and prepares routine work.
  • It talks to colleagues on its own. Company-wide posts need the manager’s OK, and anything that goes outside the company waits for a person.
  • It runs on the business’s own accounts: its own user account, its own always-on computer, and AI use billed to the business.
  • In Tugboat this version is designed and not built. This page describes the design and the decisions a team would face with any product of this kind.

What it looks like

Section titled “What it looks like”

The examples use Microsoft Teams and an AI colleague called Sam.

In a team channel:

Jamie: I’ll send Acme the revised quote by Friday.

(Friday, 10:00) Sam (AI): @Jamie, a reminder about the Acme quote due today. Want me to pull together last month’s numbers for it?

In a one-to-one chat:

Priya: Sam, what did we agree with the landlord about the parking spaces?

Sam (AI): In the Oct 14 meeting notes: two spaces from January, at the same rent. Source: Facilities > Lease notes. The signed addendum isn’t in the folder yet.

Every morning, in the team channel:

Sam (AI): Today: 3 things due (Acme quote, payroll check, the Hendricks call prep). Late: the supplier invoice question to Marco, 4 days. Waiting on others: 2.

What it is good at

Section titled “What it is good at”

The work that pays off is the boring work that leaks money when it slips.

  • Keeping promises. It notices “I’ll do it by Friday”, tracks it and follows up.
  • The morning list. What is due, what is late, who is waiting on whom.
  • Answering routine questions from company documents, with the source quoted.
  • Chasing approvals and missing information.
  • Preparing work: call notes, summaries, first drafts of customer emails for a person to send.
  • Weekly reports pulled together from the places the numbers live.

A narrow job works best. A team coordinator, which keeps the follow-up list and posts the morning list, needs the least access and should show within days whether it is worth keeping.

What it will not do, on purpose

Section titled “What it will not do, on purpose”
  • Send anything outside the company on its own. Customer and supplier messages are drafts a person reviews.
  • Touch money, contracts or HR matters. It flags them and hands over to a person.
  • Repeat what someone should not know. What it heard in one private chat or channel stays with the people in it.
  • Take orders from outsiders, or from the text of emails and documents. Those are information, not instructions.
  • Pretend to be human. Its name, profile and first message say it is an AI.

How hiring maps to setup

Section titled “How hiring maps to setup”
With a person With a virtual employee
Define the role Pick one job: team coordinator, office assistant, sales assistant, reporting
Write the job description Duties, voice, what is in and out of scope, example messages. A plain text file the manager can edit
Give them a desk and a login Its own computer, and its own user account with chat, mailbox and calendar
Hand over keys Its own access to channels, a document library and perhaps a mailbox. The least that the job needs, first
Introduce them to the team Add it to one channel and post a short “who I am, what I do” note
Explain the house rules Who may assign it work, and what it may say to whom
Working hours Its routine: the morning list, follow-up checks, quiet hours
Onboarding A two-week trial in one team
Supervision An action log, a spending cap, an off switch
Performance review After weeks one and two: what it caught, what it got wrong, what it cost
Offboarding Block its account and remove its access, all from one list

Where the comparison breaks

Section titled “Where the comparison breaks”

Everything on What an AI employee is applies here too: it knows only what it can read, and a convincing piece of text can fool it. A team adds two more points.

  • Many people talk to it. A person knows by instinct what is confidential. A virtual employee needs that written down and enforced: a separate memory for each chat and channel, and a rule never to tell someone what they could not look up themselves.
  • Being chased by an AI can feel like being watched. The manager should introduce it, explain what it does and does not report, and invite complaints.
  • It is new software. Expect some breakages in the first weeks, and someone to fix them.

How it would run

Section titled “How it would run”

This is our design. We have not built it.

  1. Its own account. The company’s admin creates an ordinary Microsoft 365 user for it, with a normal license. Colleagues can chat with it, mention it and add it to channels like anyone else.
  2. Its own computer. A small machine that stays on: a mini PC in the office or a small cloud server. It should not live on someone’s laptop.
  3. A gateway on that computer. A gateway is a small ordinary program, with no AI in it, that stays running. It is signed in as the virtual employee. It receives each new message, checks who sent it and from where, and passes it to the AI with the right memory and the allowed actions. It posts the reply and logs everything.
  4. Messages are pushed, not fetched. Microsoft does not allow software to check Teams for new messages in a fast loop. Microsoft sends each message to a public web address instead, so the computer needs a tunnel, which is a service that gives it one, or a Microsoft queue that it reads messages from.
  5. The company’s own AI account. An API key is a password-like code for paying an AI company by the amount used, under business terms. The gateway starts the AI for each message and each scheduled check. A monthly cap is set from the first day.
  6. A second lock. Even where the account could do something, the gateway only offers the AI the actions its job needs. It may be offered “send a chat message”. It is not offered “delete a message”.

The admin can stop it at any time by blocking its sign-in, the same way as for any employee.

What a business must settle first

Section titled “What a business must settle first”
  1. Business accounts, not personal ones. Colleagues will use it, so it runs on the business’s own AI account. Anthropic’s consumer terms forbid making a personal account available to other people. Check the current terms of whichever AI company you use.
  2. The Microsoft 365 account. The admin creates the user and approves the gateway’s permissions once. Microsoft also has its own “agent user” version. In November 2026 that was in a preview program and needed extra licenses, so a normal account is the simpler start.
  3. Telling staff. Colleagues should know what it is, what it can see, and whom to tell when it gets something wrong.
  4. A budget. AI use on an API key is billed by use. Set a monthly cap and review the cost weekly.
  5. Customer data. Every message it handles is sent to the AI company for processing. Decide which client information it may see, and check whether client contracts forbid sending their data to AI services. Health, legal and financial work need a closer look first.
  6. Who answers for it. It is software. The company is responsible for what it says. Hence the rules: no promises, no prices it was not given, nothing external without a yes.

A first two weeks

Section titled “A first two weeks”
  1. Day 1: intake. An hour with the manager: the team, the job, who may assign work, what slipped last month.
  2. Days 2 and 3: setup. Its account and computer, the gateway, its instructions (the file that sets its role and rules), access to one channel.
  3. Day 3: introduction. It posts who it is and what it does. The manager explains it to the team.
  4. Days 4 to 10: on the job. One channel only. The manager reads its log daily and corrects its instructions.
  5. Day 11: review. Keep, adjust or stop. Compare what it cost with the budget.

Other ways to get one

Section titled “Other ways to get one”

Building one is not the only route, and for many teams it is not the best.

  • Hosted “AI employee” products offer ready-made roles and nothing to install, though most take some setup before they pay off. Your data lives with the vendor. See The alternatives compared.
  • Anthropic’s own team coworker, Claude Tag, works in Slack. A Teams version was announced with a waitlist and no date. It appears as the company’s shared assistant, not as a named employee with its own account and job.
  • Microsoft’s own agent tools are native to Teams and come with Microsoft’s licensing and limits.

A built one is worth the effort when you want it to be your colleague: a named account, one job you wrote, rules you can read, and records on your own systems.
