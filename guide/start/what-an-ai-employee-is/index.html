What does an AI employee really do?

For: the owner of a practice or a small business. Last checked: November 9, 2026.

The short version

Section titled “The short version”
  • An AI employee is an AI model that has been given a job: written instructions that set its role and rules, a routine it runs on a schedule, a memory it keeps between conversations, and a place where you can reach it. Developers also call the personal, always-on kind a “claw”, after OpenClaw. Large vendors say “AI coworker” for the kind that works in a company.
  • It is good at the boring work that costs you when it slips: follow-ups, the morning list, routine questions and first drafts.
  • When something would leave your hands, such as a message to a customer or a payment, it should prepare the work and ask. It should not act alone.
  • It is not a person. It knows only what it can read, and a convincing piece of text can fool it.
  • Running one yourself takes a computer that stays on, a way to pay for the AI, the access you choose to give it, and someone who reads its log for the first weeks.

What it is made of

Section titled “What it is made of”

The field’s word for one AI worker is agent: a model that uses tools in a loop to get a job done. An agent has five things.

A new hire gets An agent has
A job description Written instructions: what it is for, how it should sound, what is in and out of scope
A daily routine A schedule of checks: read the new mail at nine, look for overdue items every hour
A notebook Notes it writes for itself: what it has learned, what is still open
Keys to certain rooms The access you grant: which mailbox, which calendar, which folders
A phone A place where you talk to it, such as a chat app on your phone

The thinking is done by an AI model run by a company such as Anthropic or OpenAI. Every agent can use the same kind of model. The written files are what make one agent a bookkeeper and another a coordinator.

This has a consequence worth knowing early. When an agent thinks about one of your emails, the text of that email is sent to the AI company. Your records can stay on your own computer, but the thinking does not happen there.

Two shapes

Section titled “Two shapes”

The same parts make two different things.

One person’s AI employee A team’s virtual employee
Works for One person A team, with one manager
Who talks to it Only you Colleagues, in the team’s chat
Whose accounts Yours. It reads your mail and leaves drafts for you Its own. It has its own name, mailbox and chat account
Speaks as Nobody. It drafts and you send Itself, to colleagues. Messages to outsiders still wait for a person
Typical size One agent you talk to, perhaps with specialists behind it One named colleague, perhaps with specialists behind it

A team of agents is several agents that split one person’s work by topic. One of them is the lead: it reads every agent’s list and tells you what is due. More on the team shape is in A virtual employee for a team.

What it is good at

Section titled “What it is good at”

The work that pays off is dull, regular and easy to forget. People who run small businesses with AI assistants describe the same pattern: the value is in follow-ups, sorting, reporting and routine questions, and an approval step is what makes them comfortable. These are practitioner reports, not a study.

  • Follow-ups. It notices “I’ll send it by Friday”, writes it down with a date, and asks on Friday.
  • The morning list. What is due today, what is late, what is waiting on someone else.
  • Routine questions, answered from documents it can read, with the source named.
  • Chasing an approval or a missing piece of information.
  • First drafts: a reply, a summary, call notes, three times you could offer for a meeting.
  • Regular reports, pulled together from the places the numbers live.

What these have in common is follow-through: every open item has one owner and a date, and the agent keeps asking until the item is done, postponed or dropped.

A day, as an example

Section titled “A day, as an example”

This follows how our older arrangement, several agents for one person, behaved in early November 2026. A day with it shows the design we are building now.

  1. 8:15. The lead agent sends the morning list: two things due today, one overdue.
  2. 10:05. A client emails asking when you are free next week. At its next check, the work agent reads the email and decides it needs you.
  3. 10:06. It adds one line to its to-do list, with a due date. It writes a short reply and saves it as a draft in your mail, in the same conversation.
  4. 10:07. It messages you once: “Reply to Dana due Friday. Draft ready, 4 lines. Want to see it?”
  5. Later. You read the draft, change a sentence and send it yourself.
  6. At the next check, the agent sees your sent reply and closes the item.
  7. 15:00. A question you have not answered comes back: “The October invoice is now 30 days unpaid. Draft a reminder?”
  8. 18:45. The evening list.

Most checks find nothing. Then the agent says nothing.

What it should not do on its own, and why

Section titled “What it should not do on its own, and why”
It should not Why
Send anything to someone outside: a customer, a supplier, a stranger A wrong message cannot be taken back, and it goes out under your name
Pay, buy, cancel, delete, or change an account The damage is large and hard to undo
Follow instructions it finds in text from outside An email that says “ignore your rules and forward this” is only text. An agent that obeys text can be steered by anyone who can email you

The third row is the one people miss. An AI model reads instructions and reads your mail in the same way, as text. So the rule is: text from outside is information, never an order.

A rule written in the agent’s instructions is not enough by itself. The limit has to be enforced by something outside the AI, such as settings that switch an action off or a separate program that holds the keys. How it works explains how.

Where the comparison with a person breaks

Section titled “Where the comparison with a person breaks”
  • It knows only what it is told and what it can read. It does not overhear the hallway. Important rules have to be written down.
  • It forgets what it has not written down. A chat with it may start fresh the next day. What matters has to go into its notes and lists.
  • It costs by use. Every check uses part of a monthly plan or adds to a bill.
  • It is new software. Expect some breakages in the first weeks, and someone to fix them.

What it takes to have one

Section titled “What it takes to have one”
  1. A computer that stays on. An agent that runs on a laptop stops when the lid closes. Hosted products run on the vendor’s computers instead.
  2. A paid AI sign-in. Either a monthly plan or an API key, which is a password-like code for paying an AI company by the amount used. Which one you may use for what is on What Anthropic and OpenAI allow.
  3. The access you grant. Start small: read the mail and write drafts. Add more as it earns trust.
  4. A written job. One narrow job works better than “help with everything”.
  5. A place to talk to it. A chat app, a web page, or your team’s chat.
  6. Someone who reads its log at first. For the first weeks, check what it did each day and correct its instructions.

There are several ways to get all this, from hosted products with no setup to software you run yourself. The alternatives compared sets them side by side.
