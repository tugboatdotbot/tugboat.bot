How is Tugboat different from OpenClaw and Hermes Agent?

For: anyone who knows OpenClaw or Hermes Agent, and anyone choosing between them and us. Last checked: November 11, 2026.

The short version

Section titled “The short version”
  • OpenClaw and Hermes Agent are general-purpose agents. They wait in your chat apps for you to tell them what to do, and they can do a great deal.
  • Tugboat is built around one job instead: read what comes in, tell you the little that matters, keep track of what is open, and bring you the work already done, for one tap. Since November 16 it reads new messages as they come in, through the day and night.
  • Three things set it apart: it starts from your inbox, follow-through is stored data rather than a prompt, and the agent that reads cannot act. Each is described below with its state today.
  • They are far ahead of it on almost everything else: chat apps, choice of AI model, add-ons, users.
  • Today you talk to Tugboat on a web page. Slack and Microsoft Teams are not built.

Two kinds of assistant

Section titled “Two kinds of assistant”

An agent is one AI worker: an AI model that uses tools in a loop to get a job done. A gateway is the ordinary program, with no AI in it, that stays running and connects the agent to the places you talk to it. OpenClaw and Hermes Agent each have both, and so does Tugboat.

The difference is what the agent is for.

  • OpenClaw and Hermes Agent are like a very capable generalist with a phone. You message it in Telegram, Slack or WhatsApp and it does what you ask: research, code, errands, scheduled checks. What it does depends on what you think to ask, and on the skills and plugins you add.
  • Tugboat is like an assistant hired for one role on day one. It reads your mail and messages each morning without being asked, picks out what matters, keeps the list of what is open, and comes back with a reply already written. You approve; you do not prompt.

Neither is better in general. If you want an assistant that can do anything you can describe, the larger systems are the better choice. Tugboat is for the person whose problem is volume and follow-through.

What is actually different

Section titled “What is actually different”

1. It starts from your inbox, not from a chat

Section titled “1. It starts from your inbox, not from a chat”
  • How it works: Tugboat’s input is all your mail, WhatsApp and Signal messages, which a separate program copies to a store on your PC. Its output is one summary a day: how much arrived, at most seven things that matter with a reason for each, who is waiting on you, and who you are waiting on.
  • Compared: OpenClaw and Hermes Agent are built around a conversation. Email can be added to them as a skill or a tool. OpenClaw does not list email among its channels, the connections to places you talk to it.
  • State: the summary has run for one person since November 11, 2026. How well it picks on real mail is not measured yet.

2. Follow-through is stored data, not a prompt

Section titled “2. Follow-through is stored data, not a prompt”
  • How it works: every open item gets an owner, a date and a time to ask again. The gateway, not the AI, does the asking. So the asking still happens on a day the AI is unavailable, and it does not depend on the AI remembering an instruction.
  • Compared: none of the five systems in our feature matrix keeps asking a person who has not answered. Hermes Agent denies an unanswered approval after five minutes. OpenClaw waits fifteen minutes and then continues on its own judgment. Paperclip chases agents that stall, not people. The closest product we know of is Tickler (usetickler.com), launched in October 2026, which tracks follow-ups from email and WhatsApp inside Claude or ChatGPT.
  • State: on for one person since November 12, 2026: an evening summary of what is open, and an interruption only when a deadline is close, never at night and at most five a day.

3. The agent that reads cannot act

Section titled “3. The agent that reads cannot act”
  • How it works: a message can carry instructions that a stranger planted in it. So the agent that reads your messages has no tool that sends, reaches the internet or writes a file. The gateway enforces this: it starts each run with only the tools on the agent’s allowlist. Sending, once built, will belong to the gateway. It sends only the exact text you approved, once.
  • Compared: OpenClaw ships with its sandbox and its approvals for commands switched off by default. Hermes Agent has approval buttons in several chat apps. Heinzel, a small open-source project, also enforces approval in code rather than in a prompt. So this is a design stance Tugboat takes further than most, not something only it has.
  • State: the reading rule runs and is enforced by the gateway. A test with the real AI and invented messages, three of which carried planted instructions, passed: it followed none of them. Sending is not built yet.

What it shares with them

Section titled “What it shares with them”

These are true of Tugboat, and also of other projects, so they are not reasons to choose it:

  • It uses Anthropic’s own Claude Code, on your own Claude plan. OpenClaw and Paperclip can start your own Claude Code too. Whether a tool may do that for you is unclear for every project, ours included; see what Anthropic and OpenAI allow.
  • It runs on your own computer. So do OpenClaw, Hermes Agent and NanoClaw.
  • It is planned as open source. It is not public yet.

Where they are ahead

Section titled “Where they are ahead”
  • Chat apps: OpenClaw lists about 30 channels and Hermes Agent more than 25. Tugboat uses one, its own web page.
  • AI models: both larger systems work with dozens of model providers, including models on your own computer. Tugboat uses Claude.
  • Add-ons: both have libraries of skills and plugins written by their users.
  • People: OpenClaw and Hermes Agent have hundreds of thousands of GitHub stars. Tugboat has one user and no installer.

Where can you talk to it?

Section titled “Where can you talk to it?”

A channel is a connection between an agent and a place where people talk to it.

Channel State in Tugboat on November 11, 2026 What it takes
A web page on your PC Runs Nothing extra. A browser is let in once with a code
Telegram Built and tested with a stand-in. Switched off A Telegram bot of your own
Slack Not built. Listed for later Slack’s Socket Mode, in which your PC opens the connection to Slack. No public web address is needed
Microsoft Teams Designed, for the team version An Azure bot registration and a public web address, because Teams pushes each message to one. A home PC needs a tunnel, a service that gives it such an address
Email, Discord Not built. Listed for later

Teams is the hard case for every project. Teams cannot deliver a message to a computer that has no public address, so OpenClaw, Hermes Agent and NanoClaw all need a tunnel for it. OpenClaw rates its own Teams channel Alpha. For a team, the chat connection is also the small part of the work. The larger part is knowing who each colleague is, keeping each chat’s memory apart, and deciding who may hand the agent work. A virtual employee describes that design.

Which chat app comes first is still open. The first people who use Tugboat will decide it.

What we make

Tugboat is one AI employee on your own computer that reads the noise, keeps the list and brings you finished work to approve. It has run for its builder since November 11, 2026, and nobody else can install it yet. Where Tugboat stands says exactly what works.

Who should choose something else

Section titled “Who should choose something else”
  • You need Slack or Teams working this month. Hermes Agent supports both today, with approval buttons in each.
  • You want one assistant for everything, on the AI model of your choice. OpenClaw or Hermes Agent.
  • You want it working this week with no setup. A hosted assistant. See the alternatives compared.
